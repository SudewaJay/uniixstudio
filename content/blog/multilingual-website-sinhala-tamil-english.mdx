Many Sri Lankan customers read and search in Sinhala or Tamil first. Yet most business websites here are English-only, or have a "සිංහල" button that leads to a half-translated page, a machine-translated mess or nothing at all.

A properly built multilingual website can reach people your competitors' English sites don't. A badly built one confuses customers and search engines alike. The difference comes down to a few decisions: which pages to translate, how to structure the URLs, how to tell Google the versions belong together and how to make the text look right.

This guide covers those decisions for Sri Lankan businesses adding Sinhala, Tamil or both to an English website.

## How do you build a Sinhala, Tamil and English website?

To build a multilingual website in Sri Lanka, give each language version its own URL (for example `/si/` and `/ta/`), link the versions to each other with hreflang annotations, and let visitors switch languages with a visible link rather than automatic detection. Write the content in Unicode with proper Sinhala and Tamil fonts, translate the pages customers use most first, and have a fluent person review every translation.

## Do you actually need a multilingual website?

Start with your customers, not the technology. A Sinhala or Tamil version is worth the effort when:

- Many of your customers prefer to read in Sinhala or Tamil, as is common for consumer services, healthcare, education and local retail.
- Customers ask questions by phone or WhatsApp in Sinhala or Tamil that your English site already answers.
- Your social media already performs better in Sinhala or Tamil than in English.
- You serve a region where one language clearly dominates.

It's less urgent for businesses selling mainly to corporates, exporters or foreign tourists, where English is the working language.

We've seen the language question play out in our own work. For [Bilesma Natural](/portfolio/bilesma-natural-social-media/), an Ayurvedic skin and hair care brand, headlines and benefits lead in Sinhala, the language most of its customers think in. For [RentMyCar.lk](/portfolio/rentmycar-lk/), Sinhala creative has consistently outperformed English in the account's social engagement data, so its social production is Sinhala-first. ## Decide which pages to translate first

You don't have to translate everything at once. Translating 60 pages badly is worse than translating 10 well.

Prioritise:

1. **Pages that drive enquiries:** your main services and the contact page.
2. **Practical information:** prices, preparation instructions, delivery areas, opening hours.
3. **Guides and FAQs** that answer the questions customers ask most in that language.
4. **Trust pages:** who you are, where you are and how to reach someone who speaks the language.

Leave legal pages, technical documentation and older blog posts until later, unless your customers need them.

Be honest on each translated page about what isn't translated yet. A short note saying "Our team can help you in Sinhala on WhatsApp" is better than a link that drops the reader into English without warning.

## Give every language its own URL

Google [recommends](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites) using a different URL for each language version of a page. It also advises against using cookies, browser settings or IP addresses to change the language automatically, because search engines may then only ever see one version.

That gives you three practical URL structures:

| Structure | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Subdirectories** | `example.lk/si/services/` | One domain, low maintenance. The simplest choice for most businesses. |
| **Subdomains** | `si.example.lk/services/` | Easy to set up, but users may not know what "si" means. |
| **URL parameters** | `example.lk/services/?lang=si` | Google says this isn't recommended. |

For most Sri Lankan businesses, subdirectories are the right default. Translate the URL slug too, or keep it in English if that's simpler to manage. Either works; consistency matters more.

**Avoid mixed-language pages.** Google [warns](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites) that translating only the navigation and buttons while leaving the main content in another language creates a poor experience. Each page should be in one language.

## Link the versions with hreflang

Hreflang tells search engines that pages are language versions of each other, so they can show the right one to each searcher. Google's [hreflang documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions) sets out a few rules:

- **Use ISO 639-1 language codes:** `en`, `si` for Sinhala and `ta` for Tamil. A region code such as `si-LK` is optional, but the language must come first.
- **List every version on every page, including the page itself.**
- **Make the links return.** If the English page points to the Sinhala page, the Sinhala page must point back. Annotations that aren't reciprocated may be ignored.
- **Add `x-default`** for the version to show when no language matches, usually English.
- **Pick one method:** HTML link tags in the page head, HTTP headers or the XML sitemap. You don't need all three.

For a services page in three languages, the head of each version would include:

```html
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="en" href="https://example.lk/services/" />
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="si" href="https://example.lk/si/services/" />
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="ta" href="https://example.lk/ta/services/" />
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="x-default" href="https://example.lk/services/" />
```

Each version should also have a self-referencing canonical URL. Don't point the Sinhala page's canonical at the English page, or you're telling Google to ignore it.

Google also notes that it doesn't use hreflang or the HTML `lang` attribute to detect a page's language. It works that out from the content. You should still set `lang="si"` or `lang="ta"` on each page, because screen readers and browsers use it to pronounce and render the text correctly.

## Get the typography right

Sinhala and Tamil scripts have needs that English-only websites often ignore.

**Use Unicode, never legacy fonts.** Some Sinhala text is still typed with older non-Unicode fonts, common in print design, that display Sinhala shapes over Latin characters. To a search engine, a screen reader or a phone without the font, that text is meaningless. Make sure every word on your site is real Unicode Sinhala or Tamil.

**Never put important text in images.** Text in a banner image can't be searched, translated, enlarged or read aloud. Use real text over images instead.

**Choose fonts designed for the script.** Families such as Noto Sans Sinhala and Noto Sans Tamil are free and cover the full script. Pair them with your English brand font so headings feel consistent across languages. We used Noto Sans Sinhala for RentMyCar.lk's Sinhala surfaces for exactly this reason.

**Give the text room.** Sinhala and Tamil letters have marks above and below the line, so they need more line height than English. Tamil words also tend to be longer, which can break buttons and navigation designed for short English labels. Test every component with real translated text, not placeholder text.

**Check forms.** Make sure name and address fields accept Sinhala and Tamil characters, and that validation messages are translated too.

## Translate properly

Translation quality is where most multilingual sites fail.

- **Use fluent, ideally professional, translators,** and have a second person review anything with legal, medical or financial meaning.
- **Localise, don't just translate.** Formal written Sinhala can feel stiff on a website. Match the tone your customers use, while keeping instructions precise.
- **Research how people search in each language.** Some people search in Sinhala or Tamil script, some in English, and some type Sinhala words in English letters. Your keyword research should cover all three, not assume a direct translation of your English keywords.
- **Be careful with machine translation.** It can be a starting point, but publish nothing without human review. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) list automated translation used to generate low-value pages as an example of scaled content abuse.

## Plan for maintenance

Every translated page is a page you have to keep up to date. When a price, service or phone number changes in English, it must change in Sinhala and Tamil the same day.

Before you start:

- Choose a CMS or setup that links each translation to its original, so editors can see what's out of date.
- Decide who updates each language, and how quickly.
- Add translated pages to your sitemap and check them in Google Search Console after launch.

## A launch checklist

- Pages to translate are chosen, starting with those that drive enquiries
- Each language has its own URL, preferably in a subdirectory
- Every page lists all its language versions, including itself, with hreflang
- Each version has a self-referencing canonical URL
- The `lang` attribute is set correctly on every page
- A visible language switcher links to the equivalent page, not the homepage
- All text is Unicode, with no important text in images
- Fonts support Sinhala and Tamil fully, with enough line height
- Buttons, menus and forms are tested with real translated text
- Every translation is reviewed by a fluent person
- Translated pages are in the sitemap and checked in Search Console

## The short version

A Sinhala or Tamil version of your website can reach customers who will never read the English one, but only if it's built with care. Translate the pages that matter first, give each language its own URL, connect them with hreflang, use proper Unicode fonts and have real people review the translations.
